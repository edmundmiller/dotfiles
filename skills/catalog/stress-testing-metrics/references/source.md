# Source notes

Richard W. Hamming, _The Art of Doing Science and Engineering: Learning to Learn_,
Stripe Press, 2020. Chapter 29, “You Get What You Measure,” EPUB
`OEBPS/Text/Chapter29.xhtml`. Passage anchors replace edition-dependent page numbers.

- **Opening discussion of measurement and quarterly profits:** Hamming argues
  that measurement choices influence behavior and can favor short-term results.
  We adapt this into fixing the intended outcome and its time horizon first.
- **Paragraph contrasting hard and soft measurements, before the IQ discussion:**
  he distinguishes accuracy from relevance. We use this distinction to separate
  a correctly counted score from evidence of desired progress.
- **Opening measurement metaphor and the IQ discussion:** the measurement method
  shapes the apparent result. We adapt this into inspecting reference construction
  before reaching for a hypothetical gaming example.
- **Return to computer users responding to changed ratings, after the discussion
  of measurement scales:** he asks the designer to anticipate individual responses
  to a system-wide target. We adapt this into a concrete optimization counterexample.
- **Readiness inspections and quarter-end computer shipments:** his examples
  illustrate preparation for a rating and costs shifted into a later period.
  They motivate checking controls and deferred costs; they are not independently
  verified accounts or claims about present organizations.
- **Closing lines-of-code productivity example:** he describes incentives against
  removing excess code. Our ticket-resolution example is newly constructed, not
  an example from the book.

## Adaptation and limits

The counterexample/control/correction procedure, clean-control stopping rule,
authority boundaries, ticket identity check, and explicit reference-independence
check are our design choices. Hamming does not supply this exact checklist or
prescribe our membership/derivation tests. We do not import his broad claims about
intelligence, professions, or organizational behavior as established facts.
His anecdotes motivate questions, not findings against a user or their metric.
The private book is not bundled or required at runtime.
